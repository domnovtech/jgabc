1. The intonation of the First Tone is ligated, that is, it has two notes *sol-la* joined together on the second syllable, whatever that syllable may be.
2. The flex is made by inflecting the syllable or syllables that lie between the last accent and the shorter pause by one tone [i.e., a whole tone lower], as below:
```gabc

```
3. The mediant of the First Tone is long, that is, it takes two accents, whether natural or adventitious, and is made on the separate (unjoined) notes ♮ *si la sol la*. It normally begins on the accent, whether natural or adventitious, which is the penultimate one before the pause of the mediant. The last syllable of the mediant, whatever it may be, is sung on the note *la*, which is never doubled; the syllable immediately preceding it, likewise whatever it may be, is sung on the note *sol*, which is sometimes doubled, namely if the word preceding the pause is a Latin word of at least three syllables accented on its antepenult, e.g. *dístulit*; for then the syllable marked with the accent must itself also be sung on the note *sol*. Further, the syllable or syllables occurring between the note ♮ *si* and the note *sol* are to be sung on the note *la*, e.g. *Dóminum de cœlis*, *Patri et Fílio*. And note that the terms natural accent, accent *de jure* ("by right"), grammatical accent, or prosodic accent are used interchangeably to signify one and the same thing, namely the accent which grammarians and versifiers require to be observed. An adventitious accent, or accent improperly so called, is one which an otherwise unaccented (atonic) word receives solely because of the requirements of the chant. On the penultimate adventitious accent of the mediant, that is, on unaccented words (listed on pp. 339-341) which are to be accented because of the requirements of the chant, the following is to be noted:

If the last word of the mediant is a Latin monosyllable, and it is preceded by an unaccented word of at least two syllables, the unaccented word shall be treated as accented, e.g. *súper mé*.

If the last word of the mediant is a Hebrew word of only two syllables, and it is preceded by any unaccented word whatever, the unaccented word shall be treated as accented, e.g. *éx Sión*.

If the last word of the mediant is accented on its first syllable, and it is preceded by an unaccented word of at least two syllables, the unaccented word shall be treated as accented, e.g. *súper éum*.

If the last word of the mediant has only four syllables with a short penult, or only three syllables with a long penult, and it is preceded by any unaccented word whatever, the unaccented word shall be treated as accented, e.g. *cúm princípibus*, *ét tabéscet*.

But if the last word of the mediant is a Latin word of at least five syllables with a short penult, or of at least four syllables with a long penult, or if it is a Hebrew name of at least three syllables, the second syllable before the last accent shall be treated as accented, e.g. *mísericórdia*, *bénedíctum*, *Jerúsalém*. [Tr.: In *mísericórdia* the printed accent falls on the third syllable before the last accent, not the second; the gabc examples (First, Third, and Seventh Tones: *Mi-sé-ri-cór-di-a*) place the adventitious accent on *se*, which agrees with the rule as stated. The printed accent mark in the prose example appears to be a misprint.]

But with us [i.e., in Dominican usage] an accent is never placed on the last syllable of a Latin word of two or more syllables.

What has been said here is to hold good (with the necessary distinctions being made) whenever mention of the penultimate adventitious accent occurs in the following rules.

4. There are three different terminations of the verses of the First Tone, as stated above; all of them are regularly made by descending on the separate notes *sol fa*, two syllables before the last accent. If the verse ends with a word of two or three syllables accented on its first syllable, and this final word is preceded by one or perhaps a second monosyllable (e.g. *in quibus NON EST salus*), the said monosyllables shall be considered to lack an accent, and shall be lowered onto *sol fa*. — If the last word of the verse is a monosyllable, it is not accented, but the voice is raised on *sol-la* to the accented syllable of the preceding word (e.g. *génui te*, *præcínxit se*, *consolátus és me*); indeed, if the penultimate word of the verse is of itself unaccented, it shall receive an accent (e.g. *mirabílibus súper me*, *convertúntur ád cor*). — If at the end of the verse there is a Hebrew name of two syllables (e.g. *Dávid*), the voice is raised on *sol-la* to the first syllable; but if the Hebrew name is of three or more syllables (e.g. *Jerúsalem*), the voice is raised on *sol-la* to the antepenult.

**On the Canticles of the First Tone**

8. The Gospel canticles *Magníficat* and *Benedíctus* in the First Tone begin in the same way as the psalms of the same Tone. The intonation, moreover, is made at every verse in all the Tones. No flex is made in the said canticles; a lesser pause without a flex, however, although not indicated, is permitted, as in the verses at the Introit of the Mass. (The canticle *Nunc dimíttis* at Compline, and also the canticles *Benedícite, Confíteor tibi, Ego dixi, Exultávit, Cantémus Dómino, Dómine audívi,* and *Audíte cœli,* etc., at Lauds, are sung like the psalms.)

9. The *mediant* (which is omitted at the first verse of the *Magníficat*) regularly begins by descending on the joined notes *la-sol* on the syllable that immediately precedes the penultimate accent, whether natural or adventitious (see p. 355); the penultimate accent is sung on the joined notes *sol-*♮ *si,* the penultimate syllable of the mediant on the joined notes *la-sol,* and the last syllable of the mediant on the joined notes *sol-la.* If there are any syllables between the penultimate accent and the penultimate syllable, they are to be sung on the dominant note *la.* If the last syllable of the mediant has a natural accent (accent *de jure*), the penultimate accent is taken to be the one that is in fact the antepenultimate, as in the examples *Magna qui potens est,* and *Dóminus Deus Israël.* [Tr.: On ♮ *si* see the note to §3 above; the gabc has B-flat (`gixi`).]

1. The *intonation* of the Second Tone is not ligated, but the first two syllables are sung on the separate low notes *ut re*.

2. The *flex* is made by descending on the note *re* on the syllable or syllables that lie between the last accent and the shorter pause.

3. The *mediant* of the Second Tone is called short, because it is made on the last accent only, the voice being raised to it on the note *sol.* The following syllable or syllables are sung on the dominant note *fa.* If, however, the last word of the mediant is Hebrew or a monosyllable, then, the voice having been raised to it on the note *sol,* no other note follows, e.g. *Domus Aarón, Dicta sunt de té.*

4. The psalms of the Second Tone have a single *termination,* on the separate low notes *mi ut re*, whose placement will here, for greater brevity, be explained working backwards from *re* to *ut* and from *ut* to *mi.*

¶ The last syllable of the verse is sung on the note *re,* which is never doubled.

¶ The second syllable from the end of the verse is sung on the note *ut,* which is sometimes doubled, that is, it is placed on both the second and the third syllable from the end of the verse; this happens 1) if the last word of the verse is a Latin word of at least three syllables accented on the antepenult, e.g. *íbimus,* or a Hebrew word of at least three syllables, e.g. *Israël;* 2) if the last word of the verse is a monosyllable preceded by any word of only two or three syllables, e.g. *Super me, Júdica me,* or by a word of four or more syllables accented on the penult, e.g. *adjuvábunt me.* (But if the word preceding the final monosyllable of the verse is of four or more syllables accented on the antepenult, the note *ut* is not to be doubled, e.g. *Diligéntibus se, Timéntibus se*). The note *ut* is never tripled.

If the last word of the verse, whether Latin or Hebrew, is of only two syllables, or if it is a monosyllable preceded by one or another monosyllable, the note *ut* is not doubled before the note *re,* e.g. *Mirabília fecit, Domum David, Convertúntur ad cor, Consolátus es me, Habitátio est in te.*

¶ The third syllable from the end of the verse (or the fourth, if the note *ut* is to be doubled) is sung on the note *mi.* This note *mi* is doubled if the word to which that note *mi* belongs is of at least three syllables accented on the antepenult, and in addition the immediately following word begins on the note *ut,* e.g. *Dómini íbimus, fíliæ Sion, Sæculo tu es.* In other cases the note *mi* is not doubled before the note *ut,* even if the note *ut* is preceded by two syllables, even ones lacking an accent, that do not belong to one and the same word, e.g. *Pestiléntiæ non sedit, Pedum tuórum, Fácere cum eis.*

2. There are two terminations of the verses of the Third Tone, as noted above. [Tr.: So numbered in the source; by sequence this would be §4 (and the "3." below would be §5).]

The first *termination* of the Third Tone begins on the fifth or on the fourth syllable before the end of the verse, on the joined notes *ut-si,* except in two cases to be given below, in which it begins on the separate note *si.*

First, the first termination of the Third Tone begins on the joined notes *ut-si* on the fifth syllable from the end of the verse if the last word of the verse is accented on its antepenult, or is a Hebrew word of at least three syllables, e.g. *Anima mea Dóminum, In lege Dómini, Ordinem Melchísedech, Tuis Jerúsalem.* — Second, the first termination of the Third Tone begins on the joined notes *ut-si* on the fifth syllable from the end of the verse if the last word of the verse is a monosyllable, and the second word from the end of the verse is any word of at least two syllables, e.g. *Et præcinxit se, Meam super me, Ante lucíferum génui te, Diligéntibus te, Vivífica me* (Exception: the first termination of the Third Tone does indeed begin on the fifth syllable from the end of the verse, but on the note *si* alone, when the second word from the end of the verse is accented on its antepenult and the last word of the verse is of only three syllables accented on its antepenult, e.g. *Erigens páuperem, Dómini íbimus.* — The same happens if the third word from the end of the verse is of at least three syllables accented on its antepenult, while the second word from the end of the verse is of only two syllables and the last word is a monosyllable, e.g. *In mirabílibus super me.* [Tr.: The source reads "Exceptionem" (accusative, perhaps for "Exceptio" or "[Nota] exceptionem") and the parenthesis is never closed. Note also that the first exception (*Erigens páuperem*) actually modifies the "First" case (final word accented on the antepenult), not the "Second" case inside whose parenthesis it is printed; the second exception modifies the "Second" case. The gabc confirms both: the fifth syllable from the end is on the single note *si* in *E-ri-gens páuperem*, *Dó-mi-ni íbimus*, and *In mirabí-li-bus super me*.]

¶ But on the fourth syllable before the end of the verse, and likewise on the joined notes *ut-si,* the first termination of the Third Tone begins in the cases not covered above.

Further, once the termination has begun, whether on the joined notes *ut-si* or on the separate note *si,* as above, the first syllable that follows is always sung on the joined notes *la-si,* the next on the separate note *la,* which is never doubled, and finally the last syllable of the verse on the joined notes *sol-la.* Sometimes, however, a separate note *sol* is sung between the note *la* and the final notes *sol-la,* namely 1) if the last word of the verse is of at least three syllables accented on its antepenult, e.g. *Dóminum,* 2) if the last word of the verse is a Hebrew word of at least three syllables, e.g. *Jerusalém;* 3) if the last word is a monosyllable preceded by a word of at least two syllables, e.g. *Præcínxit se, Super me, Ante lucíferum génui te.*

3. The *second termination* or *differentia* of the Third Tone begins sometimes by descending on the note *la* alone on the syllable before the last accent, sometimes by descending on the separate notes *si la* on the two syllables that precede it.

But first it must be noted what accent is, or is here to be considered, the last. If the last word of the verse is a monosyllable, the last accent shall be considered to be the accent of the preceding word, which, if it lacks a natural accent, shall receive an adventitious accent because of the requirements of the chant, as below in the examples *Tuam vivífica me. Convertúntur ád cor,* and *Mirabílibus súper me.* — When there is a Hebrew name at the end of the verse, it is accented on the penult if it is a name of two syllables, but on the antepenult if of three or more syllables, as below in the examples *Deo Jácob.* and *Tuis Jerúsalem.* — If the last word of the verse is of two or three syllables accented on the first syllable, and it is preceded by a monosyllable or a Hebrew name, the said monosyllable or the said Hebrew name shall lose its accent, as below in the examples *In quibus non est salus* and *Pestiléntiæ non sedit.* [Tr.: Both examples of this last rule involve only a monosyllable (*est*, *non*); no example of a preceding Hebrew name is given. With the last accent thus defined, the following points are to be noted about the second termination of the Third Tone.

The *second termination* of the Third Tone begins by descending on the note *la* on the syllable that precedes the last accent: First, if that syllable stands alone between the penultimate and the last accent, as below in the examples *Dextris meis, Lege Dómini* and *Consolátus és mé.* — Second, the second termination of the Third Tone begins by descending on the note *la* on the syllable that precedes the last accent if the last word of the verse is a Latin word of only three syllables accented on the penult, or a Latin word of only four syllables accented on the antepenult, or also a Hebrew word of only four syllables, and it is preceded by an otherwise unaccented monosyllable (which, because of the requirements of the chant, shall not be considered unaccented), as below in the examples *In excélsis, In lætítia, In Jerúsalem.* — Third, the second termination of the Third Tone begins by descending on the note *la* on the syllable that precedes the last accent if the last word of the verse is of at least four syllables with a long penult, or of at least five syllables with a short penult, as below in the examples *Esuriéntibus,* and *Populórum.*

¶ But if, outside the preceding cases, there are two or more syllables between the penultimate and the last accent, the termination is to begin by descending on the separate notes *si la* on the two syllables that precede the last accent, as below in the examples *Pestiléntiæ non sedit, Spirítui Sancto* and *Tuis Jerúsalem.*

The last accent, and the syllable or syllables between it and the last syllable of the verse, are sung on *ut,* according to the examples *E u ó u a e, Lége Dómini, Tuum vivífica me,* etc.

The last syllable of the verse is sung on the notes *si-la,* joined, as below in all the examples.

The *termination* of the psalms of the Fifth Tone is single, and takes two accents, whether natural or adventitious.

¶ The PENULTIMATE adventitious accent in the termination of the verses of the Fifth Tone is found as follows: 1) If the last word of the verse is a Latin word of only three syllables accented on the penult, or a Latin word of only four syllables accented on the antepenult, or a Hebrew word of four syllables, and it (namely the last word of the verse) is preceded by an unaccented monosyllable, that monosyllable shall be accented because of the requirements of the chant, and shall be considered to bear the penultimate accent, e.g. *In excélsis, In lætítia, In Jerúsalem.* 2) If the last word of the verse is a Latin word of at least four syllables accented on the penult, the syllable that is fourth from the end of the verse shall be considered to bear the penultimate accent, e.g. *Pópulórum.* 3) If the last word of the verse is a Latin word of at least five syllables accented on the antepenult, the syllable that is fifth from the end of the verse shall be considered to bear the penultimate accent, e.g. *Esúriéntibus.*

¶ The LAST adventitious accent in the termination of the verses of the Fifth Tone is found as follows: 1) If the last word of the verse is a monosyllable, it shall be reckoned unaccented, and the preceding word shall be considered to bear the last accent, even if it is of itself unaccented, e.g. *ád cor, súper me.* 2) When there is a Hebrew name at the end of the verse, it shall be accented on the penult if it is of two syllables, e.g. *Dávid, Jácob;* but on the antepenult if of more, e.g. *Israël, Jerúsalem.* 3) If the last word of the verse is of two or three syllables accented on the first syllable, and it is preceded by an accented monosyllable, the said monosyllable shall lose its accent, as below in the examples *In quibus non* EST *salus* and *Pestiléntiæ* NON *sedit.*

Further, the penultimate accent in the verses of the psalms of the Fifth Tone is made by raising the voice to it on high *re*, and the last accent is made on the dominant note *ut.* If there is one syllable between the penultimate and the last accent, it is sung on high *si*, e.g. *Dextris meis;* if two syllables, the first is sung on the dominant note *ut,* the second on the note *si,* e.g. *Mirabília fecit;* if three syllables, the first two are sung on the dominant note *ut,* the third on the note *si,* e.g. *Pestiléntiæ non sedit.* The syllable or syllables that follow the last accent are sung on the note *la.*

6. Last word of the verse a monosyllable, preceded by a word of AT LEAST FOUR syllables, accented on the antepenult, e.g. *Diligéntibus te, Et enútries me, Vivífica me* (Note that this sixth case differs from the following, the seventh). [Tr.: "a sequenti septimum" is grammatically odd (one would expect "a sequenti septimo"); the sense is clear.]

7. Last word of the verse a monosyllable, preceded by a word of ONLY THREE syllables, accented on the antepenult, e.g. *Ante lucíferum génui te, Tua líbera me, Ut éruas me.*

8. Last word of the verse a monosyllable, preceded by a word of two or three (but not more) syllables having a natural or adventitious accent on the penult, e.g. *Et præcínxit se, In mirabílibus super me, Quæ in eis sunt.*

9. Last word a monosyllable, preceded by one or another monosyllable, e.g. *Qui convertúntur ad cor, Consolátus es me, Habitátio est in te.*

4. There are two terminations or differentiae of the verses of the Eighth Tone, as noted above.

¶ The first termination of the Eighth Tone is made on the separate notes *si ut la sol.* It thus has only one preparatory note, *si,* before the last, culminating note, high *ut*, which is followed by two separate notes, one lower than the other, namely *la sol*; the latter is sometimes doubled. [Tr.: "quæ aliquando geminatur" is singular and so refers to *sol*, not to both notes; the gabc confirms that only *sol* is doubled.]

¶ The second termination of the Eighth Tone has two separate preparatory notes, *la ut,* before the culminating note *re,* which is followed by the note *ut,* which is sometimes to be doubled, and even tripled.

The *first termination* of the verses of the Eighth Tone begins on the note *si* on the fifth or on the fourth syllable before the end of the verse.

¶ It begins on the fifth syllable from the end of the verse: First, if the last word of the verse is a Latin word accented on the antepenult, e.g. *In domum Dómini íbimus,* or a Hebrew name of at least three syllables, e.g. *Tuis Jerúsalem.* – Second, the first termination begins on the fifth syllable before the end of the verse if the last word of the verse is a monosyllable preceded by a Latin word of at least two syllables, e.g. *In mirabílibus super me, Lucíferum génui te, Abundántia diligéntibus te* and *Vivífica me.* In which case the fifth syllable from the end of the verse is sung on the note *si,* the fourth on the note *ut,* the third on the note *la,* the remaining two on the note *sol.* [Tr.: "In which case" is printed after the Second case, but the note assignment applies to both five-syllable cases; the gabc (*In domum Dómini íbimus*, *Tuis Jerúsalem*) follows the same pattern.]

¶ But on the fourth syllable from the end of the verse the first termination of the verses of the Eighth Tone begins in the cases not covered above; and then the fourth syllable from the end of the verse is sung on the note *si,* the third on the note *ut,* the second on the note *la,* the last on the note *sol.*

The second *termination* of the Eighth Tone, which is made on the separate notes *la ut re ut,* begins sometimes on the sixth, sometimes on the fifth, sometimes on the fourth syllable before the end.

¶ The second termination of the Eighth Tone begins on the sixth syllable before the end of the verse if the last word of the verse is a monosyllable preceded by a word of at least three syllables accented on the antepenult, e.g. *Lucíferum génui te, Persecúti sunt me ádjuva me, Tua vivífica me, Abundántia diligéntibus te.* Then the sixth and fifth syllables before the end of the verse are sung on the separate notes *la ut,* the fourth on the culminating note *re,* the remaining three on the dominant note *ut,* which is tripled.
